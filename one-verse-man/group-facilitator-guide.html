DON'T MISS YOUR VERSE

A church-ready small-group teaching and facilitator guide

TheWrkShop.com | The One Verse Man

Core message: Some men are known for chapters. Others are known for moments. God uses both.

Purpose: Help participants notice how attention, desires, fear, pride, and habits affect their response to ordinary opportunities for love and obedience. The goal is not to seize every interruption, but to grow in Christlike discernment, humble service, and grace-rooted faithfulness.

Audience: Men's ministries, mixed adult small groups, church leadership groups. Adjust examples for your setting. Format: 45 minutes (core) or 75 minutes (extended). Ideal breakouts: 3–5 participants.

Leader preparation

Read Luke 10:25–37, James 1:22–25, Philippians 2:3–8, John 15:4–5, and Ephesians 2:8–10 in context. Optional character studies: Shamgar (Judges 3:31; 5:6), Susanna (Luke 8:1–3), Simon of Cyrene (Mark 15:21; Luke 23:26), Ebed-Melech (Jeremiah 38:7–13; 39:15–18), Peter (Luke 22:54–62; John 21:15–19).

Exegetical boundaries: Simon was compelled to carry the cross; Scripture does not report his motives or conversion. Luke names Susanna among women supporting Jesus' ministry; her personal motivations are not disclosed. Shamgar also appears in Judges 5:6. Avoid treating biblical characters as psychological case files. Jesus' atoning cross is unique; not every hardship is a cross God requires a person to carry.

Safety and care: Explain confidentiality but do not promise absolute secrecy: leaders must follow their church's safeguarding policies and applicable reporting duties. No participant is required to disclose diagnostic scores, trauma, or personal history. Never pressure participants to reconcile with an abuser or stay in an unsafe situation. Encourage appropriate pastoral, professional, or emergency help where warranted. Avoid using the exercise to assess spiritual worth or salvation.

45-minute meeting plan

Time Segment Facilitator action
0–5 CONNECT Chat or spoken warm-up: “What is one small act of kindness you still remember years later?”
5–15 WORD Teach the core script below.
15–19 SEARCH Quiet reflection: select one pattern and one actual situation.
19–37 BROTHERHOOD Groups of 3–5 discuss 3 questions.
37–42 RESPONSE Each person identifies one wise step and optional accountability.
42–45 SEND Closing prayer and invitation to seven-day practice.

Facilitator teaching script (approximately 10 minutes)

“Many of us think spiritual significance must look impressive. We imagine a season when we will have more time, more influence, more confidence, or a larger platform. But much of our character is formed in ordinary responses: how we listen, how we handle correction, whether we tell the truth, how we respond when someone needs us, and whether we can serve without being noticed.

“Consider the Good Samaritan in Luke 10:25–37. The lawyer asks Jesus about inheriting eternal life and then asks, ‘Who is my neighbor?’ Jesus tells a story in which a wounded man is passed by, but a Samaritan sees him and acts with compassion. Jesus ends not by asking us to admire compassion, but by calling for neighbor-love in action. Notice that the passage is not a command to respond to every demand without discernment. It is a challenge to the boundaries we place around love.

“James 1:22–25 confronts a different danger: hearing the Word without doing it. We can accumulate sermons, podcasts, and Bible studies while neglecting a conversation or responsibility we already know requires attention. Knowledge is a gift, but knowledge alone is not the same as practiced obedience.

“Philippians 2:3–8 presses deeper: What motivates us? Do we serve because we love, or because the assignment makes us feel important? Do we want another person to flourish, or do we need to be the person who gets credit? The humility of Christ confronts our craving for recognition.

“Think about an everyday example. A husband comes home exhausted. His family needs attention. His fatigue is real; rest may be necessary. But he notices that his first response to a request is resentment. The question is not ‘Must I say yes to everything?’ The better question is ‘What story am I telling myself, and what would love and wisdom require?’ He may need to rest, explain that kindly, and follow through on a promise. Or he may need to put down his phone and listen. The faithful response is discerned, not assumed.

“Here is the deeper battle: A moment appears. We interpret it. Something we desire—comfort, control, recognition, approval—may feel threatened. We choose whether to examine that desire under God's Word or let it govern our response. Not every feeling is sin, and not every delay is avoidance. But our interpretations deserve scrutiny.

“Finally, remember John 15:4–5: Jesus calls us to abide in Him. This is not a challenge to earn God's love by catching every opportunity. Ephesians 2:8–10 teaches salvation by grace through faith, with good works as the fruit of God's work in us. When we fail, we can repent and return to Christ. The goal is not perfect performance; it is a life increasingly shaped by His love.

“So here is our question tonight: What is one ordinary moment in your life where the Lord may be calling you to a more faithful response—and what inside you has been resisting it?”

Quiet reflection (4 minutes)

Invite each participant to write privately:

  1. A recent situation where I was distracted, defensive, fearful, resentful, or concerned about recognition.
  2. The fact of what happened, separate from the story I told myself about it.
  3. The desire, fear, or legitimate need underneath my response.
  4. What Scripture, wisdom, and love suggest as an appropriate next step.

Breakout discussion (18 minutes)

Question A — Recognition: “Where have you wanted to do something good, but struggled because the contribution felt too small, unseen, or unappreciated?” Ask for a specific example. Follow-up: “Was your need for appreciation legitimate, or had recognition become a condition of serving?”

Question B — Resistance: “Think of a responsibility or conversation you have delayed. Is the delay due to genuine limitations, the need for discernment, or fear and self-protection?” Follow-up: “What evidence would help you tell the difference?”

Question C — Response: “What is one appropriate act of listening, truth-telling, service, repair, or boundary-setting you can take within seven days?” Follow-up: “What might make it difficult, and who could support you?”

Leader prompts: “Can you name a real situation?” “What did you assume?” “What were you protecting?” “What would a wise boundary look like?” “Where does grace enter this?” Do not interrogate or pressure disclosure. Avoid making speculative claims about God's secret intentions.

Closing practice (5 minutes)

Invite participants to complete privately:

  • My moment: __________________________
  • The resistance I notice: __________________________
  • What faithfulness may require: __________________________
  • One step I will take, and when: __________________________
  • A person I may invite to check in: __________________________

Closing prayer: “Father, search our hearts. Where we have overlooked people, teach us to love. Where pride or fear has governed us, lead us to repentance. Where we are exhausted or carrying too much, grant wisdom, rest, and support. Help us abide in Christ rather than trying to prove our worth. Give us discernment for the moments before us and grace to return when we fail. In Jesus' name, amen.”

75-minute extended version

Add 10 minutes for participants to complete or review the online diagnostic privately, 10 minutes for the case studies below, and 10 minutes for a second round of group discussion and prayer. Do not collect individual scores.

Three case studies for discussion

Case 1 — The overlooked volunteer. A faithful volunteer is disappointed when a newer person is asked to lead publicly. He starts criticizing the new leader. Discuss: What is a legitimate concern? What might be envy? What does Philippians 2 require? How can leaders practice genuine appreciation?

Case 2 — The exhausted caregiver. A man caring for an ill parent has become short-tempered and refuses offers of help. Discuss: What is a genuine burden? What is his responsibility? What support and boundaries are appropriate? Read Galatians 6:2–5 without reducing it to a simplistic formula.

Case 3 — The delayed apology. A supervisor realizes he embarrassed a team member publicly but keeps explaining his intentions rather than apologizing. Discuss: What fact is he avoiding? What fear is active? What would repentance and repair look like? Consider James 1:19–22.

Seven-day follow-through

Day 1: Notice one overlooked responsibility (James 1:22). Day 2: Serve appropriately without seeking applause (Matthew 6:1–4). Day 3: Name a burden and seek wise support (Galatians 6:2–5). Day 4: Practice courage with discernment (Jeremiah 38:7–13). Day 5: Identify a desire competing with obedience (Mark 10:17–22). Day 6: Practice confession and restoration (John 21:15–19). Day 7: Review, pray, and choose one sustainable habit (John 15:4–5).

Using the diagnostic with your group

Share the companion HTML assessment link in advance or during the meeting. It uses 35 self-reported statements across seven dimensions, each scored 5–25. Higher scores mean the participant reported those obstacles more frequently; they do not diagnose a condition or establish maturity. Results stay in the browser unless the participant downloads, prints, or chooses to email them to himself with the optional 7-day plan. Do not rank participants or use results for hiring, ministry eligibility, discipline, or counseling decisions.
