When God seems silent

Feeling forsaken isn't the same as being forsaken. Even Jesus prayed the question.

The Revelation

You prayed.

Then you prayed again.

Months. Maybe years.

The diagnosis didn't change.
The marriage didn't heal.
The son didn't come home.
The job didn't open.

And heaven feels like a ceiling.

Here's what most men don't know:

The Bible has prayers for exactly this.

And God put them there on purpose.

Four Times “How Long?”

How long, LORD? Will you forget me forever? How long will you hide your face from me? How long must I wrestle with my thoughts and day after day have sorrow in my heart? How long will my enemy triumph over me?Psalm 13:1–2

Count them.

Four times in two verses.

How long. How long. How long. How long.

This is in your Bible.

Not as a warning about weak faith.

As a prayer. For you to pray.

Notice who David is talking to.

He isn't complaining about God to his friends.

He's complaining to God.

Lament isn't the opposite of faith. It's faith that refuses to stop talking to God.

Then the turn:

But I trust in your unfailing love; my heart rejoices in your salvation. I will sing the LORD's praise, for he has been good to me.Psalm 13:5–6

Look closely.

Nothing in the psalm says his situation changed.

What changed is where he looked.

He remembered: “He has been good to me.”

The Psalm That Doesn't Resolve

Now read Psalm 88.

It starts like this:

LORD, you are the God who saves me; day and night I cry out to you.Psalm 88:1

And it ends like this:

You have taken from me friend and neighbor—darkness is my closest friend.Psalm 88:18

That's the last line.

No turn.

No “but I will trust.”

No bow on top.

Most psalms of lament move toward praise.

Psalm 88 just stops in the dark.

And God kept it in the Bible.

Why?

Because some nights don't resolve.

And God wanted you to know you can still pray on those nights.

Even here, look at the first line again: “the God who saves me.”

In the darkest psalm, he still calls God Savior.

You're allowed to end a prayer without a happy ending. Psalm 88 does.

The Answer Paul Didn't Ask For

Three times I pleaded with the Lord to take it away from me. But he said to me, “My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness.”2 Corinthians 12:8–9

Three times.

Same as Jesus in Gethsemane.

Paul asked for removal.

He got presence instead.

The thorn stayed.

Grace came.

And notice what Paul never tells us: what the thorn was.

People have guessed for two thousand years.

Nobody knows.

Which means nobody gets to tell you what your thorn means, either.

The Lie

“If God were listening, something would have changed.”

And right behind it:

“I must have done something wrong.”

Job's friends said that.

They sat with him for seven days.

Then they opened their mouths and ruined it.

God's verdict on their explanations? They had not spoken the truth about Him (Job 42:7).

Yes, examine your heart. Repentance is always good.

But unanswered prayer is not proof of hidden sin.

The Truth

Feeling forsaken isn't the same as being forsaken.

Silence isn't absence.
“Not yet” isn't “no.”
No explanation isn't no love.

You don't have to understand why to keep talking to Him.

Look at Jesus

Friday. Three in the afternoon.

And at three in the afternoon Jesus cried out in a loud voice, “Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?” (which means “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?”).Mark 15:34

Jesus prayed the question.

He prayed it from Psalm 22:1, a lament.

The Son of God knows exactly what it feels like when heaven goes quiet.

But listen to the words: “My God.”

Even forsaken, He still said my.

Then came Saturday.

A whole day of silence.

The disciples hiding. The tomb sealed. Nothing happening.

From where they sat, God had gone completely quiet.

He hadn't.

Sunday was coming.

Jesus went into God's silence so that silence would never get the last word over you.

And He knows how it feels:

For we do not have a high priest who is unable to empathize with our weaknesses…Hebrews 4:15

Upstream: What You Conclude

The silence isn't what breaks most men.

It's what we conclude from it.

  1. I asked
  2. Nothing changed
  3. I decide what that means
  4. I stop asking

Separate the two:

What happened: I asked for healing. It hasn't come.
What I concluded: God doesn't care about me.

The first is a fact.

The second is a story you're telling yourself.

And here's the real danger:

When God seems silent, men go silent back.

We stop praying.
We stop telling anyone.
We just carry it.

The danger isn't God's silence. It's ours.

The Audit

  • What have I asked for that hasn't come?
  • What have I concluded about God from that?
  • What have I concluded about myself?
  • Have I stopped praying about it, or just stopped saying it out loud?
  • Who knows I'm carrying this?

Then:

Have I gone quiet on God because I thought He went quiet on me?

The Practice

Write your own Psalm 13.

Four lines that start with “How long…”

Be honest. He can take it.

Then decide how to end it.

Maybe with David: “But I trust in your unfailing love.”
Or maybe with Psalm 88, and leave it unfinished tonight.

Both are prayers.

Then tell one man what you've been carrying.

You don't need him to explain it.

You need him to sit with you.

If the darkness has stopped lifting, or you've started thinking about not being here, tell someone today. In the U.S., call or text 988 any time. If you're in danger right now, call 911.

Take It With You

Feeling forsaken isn't the same as being forsaken.

The Prayer

Father,

How long?
I've asked, and I've waited,
and I don't understand.

I'm not going to pretend this doesn't hurt.
I'm not going to make up a reason.
I'm just going to keep talking to You.

Keep me from deciding You don't care.
Keep me from going silent on You.
Keep me from carrying this alone.

Jesus, You prayed “Why?” from the cross.
You know this place.
Meet me in it.

Even here, You are my God.
In Jesus' name,
Amen.
